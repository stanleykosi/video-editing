# Sound Design

## Purpose

Help the agent make edits feel intentional through audio texture, transitions,
impact, ambience, intelligibility, and mix control.

## When To Use This Skill

- Adding whooshes, risers, hits, ambience, room tone, or music beds.
- Repairing harsh cuts or empty-feeling transitions.
- Mixing dialogue, testimony, source sound, SFX, and music.
- Choosing or mixing music as a retention, tone, or structure tool.
- Repairing documentary audio or deciding whether subtitles/dubbing are needed.
- Using music to characterize protagonist confidence, failure, learning, or mastery.
- Designing sensory sound worlds for documentary places, materials, fantasy sequences, and grounded resets.
- Auditioning licensed music to repair generic, cheap, flat, or tonally wrong scenes.
- Bridging music from in-world source sound into full-score needle drops.
- Building clean dialogue/music/SFX track hygiene for YouTube edits.
- Adding SFX variation without burying speech or cheapening tone.
- Designing high-energy short-form whooshes, hits, reverse hits, and low music beds.
- Auditioning social short-form music under a finished Reel to choose tone fit without masking speech.
- Finishing SFX and music after an After Effects animation render is aligned in Premiere.
- Running a music-first voiceover assembly or isolated SFX spotting pass for scripted YouTube edits.
- Adding realistic foley and contrast drops to slower human short-form edits without overwhelming dialogue.
- Syncing restrained SFX and music to editorial infographic motion without overpowering narration.
- Finishing Resolve Fairlight mixes with dialogue channel mapping, ambience,
  SFX passes, music splices, buses, EQ, and dialogue dynamics.
- Designing frame-locked SFX and music beds for premium brand, show, podcast, course, or social-series intros.
- Automating music volume with keyframes so montage sections feel full and speech stays clear.
- Pairing music volume keyframes with visual filter-strength reveals only after
  speech clears.
- Spotting anime/action edit SFX and short voice lines against locked visual hits, transitions, and character moments.
- Matching CapCut anime SFX to exact event families such as eye transformations, lightning entrances, glass-break overlays, and identity voice lines.
- Licensing and syncing music/SFX for CapCut pro effects, green-screen VFX,
  clone illusions, sticker pops, and slide/card transitions.
- Syncing tiny ticks/clicks to CapCut text flickers, font shifts, number counters,
  and outline reveals without masking speech.
- Syncing short glitch, shimmer, or tick cues to premium CapCut highlight wipes,
  glow sweeps, and staggered text reveals.
- Adding restrained risers, ambience, and texture cues to documentary research,
  archival, filmed-screen, and parallax graphics.

## Core Principles

- Sound should explain or sell a visual change.
- Bad sound can break a video faster than imperfect visuals when speech carries story.
- Speech and testimony remain the priority unless the user explicitly asks for music-first style.
- Use impacts sparingly so big moments stay big.
- Use ambience consistently across related B-roll sequences.
- Confirm licensing or platform safety before using mainstream or copyrighted tracks.
- Do not rely on a cool song to compensate for weak structure.
- Choose music for character meaning, not only energy.
- Sound can characterize a world, material, or conflict before the VO names it.
- A pause beat is useful when image plus sound can communicate more cleanly than another explanatory line.
- Music supervision includes track auditioning, cue timing, filtering, ducking, exit planning, and license safety.
- A famous or premium track is only valuable when it fits the story better than the alternatives.
- Keep dialogue, music, ambience, and SFX controllable on separate tracks or logical groups.
- Use music to enhance a strong cut, not to disguise weak pacing.
- Repeated SFX should vary and should land on real motion, impact, or reveal frames.
- Text SFX should end with the visible text change; a tick that continues after a
  flicker or font shift feels detached.
- Staggered text reveal SFX should begin on the first visible word change and end
  on the final reveal frame.
- In dense short-form edits, SFX should make motion feel alive while speech remains dominant.
- Reversed hits can build anticipation into a real question, reveal, or transition.
- Trend status is not a substitute for story fit, mix clarity, or license/platform safety.
- In AE-heavy Reels, sound is a finishing pass that should lock to visible motion, not a decoration added before animation timing is known.
- Temporarily muting other tracks can help spot SFX, but the restored full mix decides whether the SFX actually works.
- Slow human shorts often need believable physical texture more than loud template SFX.
- Contrast can be built with sound and cut density, then released into silence, room tone, or calmer ambience.
- Documentary infographic SFX should clarify hierarchy, not turn maps/charts into noisy social motion.
- In Fairlight, think in clips, tracks, and buses: edit the clip when the fix is
  local, the track when a sound family needs control, and the bus when grouped
  processing or shared volume is needed.
- Premium intro sound should sell visible logo, text, flicker, underline, camera, cut, or transition events; unsynced decorative cues make the intro feel cheaper.
- Proof quotes and first body lines stay dominant even when intro music and SFX raise perceived production value.
- Music ducking should begin before speech starts; if the first word is masked, the keyframe is too late.
- Numeric duck values are starting points, not rules; the actual song, voice, and
  phone-speaker intelligibility decide the final level.
- In anime/action edits, SFX and voice lines should identify or sell a visible beat; contrast disappears if every clip gets the same intensity.
- Similar-looking intense moments still need distinct cue families when the visible event is different.
- In-app music/SFX libraries, downloaded green-screen effects, and screen-recorded
  cues still need license or platform-safety status before delivery.
- Documentary research risers should create investigation and anticipation only
  when the sequence resolves into a real discovery, proof frame, or claim.

Contradiction note: serious documentary usually prefers restrained music and real ambience. A beautiful or nostalgic track over horrific imagery can be stronger when it creates grief, critique, or haunting irony, but reject it if it glamorizes harm or feels too far for the actual story.

## Timing Rules

- Duck music and SFX before story-critical words begin, not after they are already masked.
- Let music or ambience carry observational long-form sections when dialogue would over-explain.
- Plan the exit, duck, or transition from a high-energy track before the next story beat.
- If a cut uses a tone shift into comedy or relief, make the sound cue deliberate rather than cheesy.
- Shift music tone when the protagonist moves from overconfidence to humbling or from learning to competence.
- A 1-2 second sound-image pause can let the viewer absorb scale, conflict, or unreality without VO.
- Abrupt silence works best after a ramped sequence and should land on grounded natural sound or a clear reality image.
- Align a needle-drop cue or downbeat to a meaningful visual turn, not the nearest convenient cut.
- If music starts as a source cue, open it into full mix on an action turn, wide shot, reveal, or section shift.
- For auditions, compare candidate tracks against the same cue point before judging story fit.
- For short-form music selection, test candidate tracks under the finished edit and reject tracks that change the emotion in the wrong direction.
- Repair quiet words with local gain or volume keyframes rather than raising an entire clip by default.
- Remove or smooth accidental dead air in tight talking-head edits unless the pause has purpose.
- Hits or whooshes should land within 1-2 frames of the visual impact when sync matters.
- Reverse hits should peak into a real reveal, question, or transition, not an ordinary word.
- Background music should stay low enough that captions are support, not rescue.
- Place whooshes, hits, typewriter sounds, camera sounds, and reward cues after the AE render is aligned so cues land on actual visible motion.
- Align the strongest music phrase to a meaningful animated or story beat, then split or duck music under dialogue-heavy sections.
- If music is added early after voiceover, treat it as a vibe and beat guide until final mix and license checks pass.
- Isolated SFX spotting must be followed by a restored full-mix check.
- For short-form contrast drops, keep black/silence gaps under one second unless the pause is intentionally cinematic.
- Human-scene foley should sync to visible movement and sit below dialogue.
- Infographic SFX should be placed after route, chart, highlight, and screenshot timing is locked.
- In Fairlight, add obvious story SFX first, then layer secondary body/object
  texture, and only judge levels after restoring the full mix.
- For precision intro hits, land cues within 0-1 frames of the visible logo, text, underline, flicker, camera, or transition event.
- A fast intro can start from a 120 BPM-style pulse when it fits the brand, then adjust to speaker pace and proof readability.
- Duck, fade, or cut the intro bed over the final 4-12 frames when body speech begins immediately.
- In CapCut-style volume automation, place the low-volume keyframe before speech begins and restore only after the phrase or talking section clears.
- As a starting range for CapCut-style music ducking, test around -15 dB to -20
  dB under speech, or around -14 dB for a softer track, then adjust by ear.
- When a filter-strength reveal is paired with music, restore music and raise the
  look only after the phrase clears.
- Spot anime/action SFX after visual timing is locked enough to place hits on real motion, impact, reveal, or transition frames.
- Place clip-entrance cues on the first readable character/effect frame and place power-overlay cues on the reveal or lock frame.
- Short identity voice lines should start at or just before the clip's opening beat only when they clarify character or emotion.
- Place explosion, sticker, chapter-card, and clone foley cues after the visual
  impact or action frame is locked.
- Place flicker, font-shift, and number-counter ticks only after text animation
  timing is locked, then trim or speed them so their tails clear with the visual.
- Place glitch or shimmer cues for staggered reveals and glow sweeps only after
  mask/reveal timing is locked, then trim the cue to the visible effect span.
- In research overlays, place one restrained riser or low bed after visual
  timing is set, and fade it before the next narration-heavy claim.

## Motion Rules

- Sound accents should match the motion they support.
- Do not add whooshes to mature documentary transitions unless they match the tone.
- Use SFX only where they sell a motion, impact, reveal, comedy beat, or transition.
- Rotate whoosh and hit variants in repeated social edits.
- Pair whooshes with actual text, icon, zoom, or transition movement.
- Anime/action whooshes, hits, textures, glitches, and voice lines should map to visible movement, impact, transformation, or character identity beats.
- Eye, lightning, glass, and voice-line cues should be selected for their visible event, not reused just because they feel intense.

## Sound Rules

- Try source selection, dialogue cleanup, volume automation, subtitles, then dubbing/paraphrase if ethical.
- Music must fit both retention goals and subject tone.
- For war, grief, crisis, or testimony, prefer restraint over upbeat stock energy.
- If one B-roll shot has audible street or people sound, decide whether all related B-roll needs ambience.
- SFX peaks should not overpower dialogue or testimony.
- Music should not flatten all story phases into one emotional tone.
- Give repeated story worlds distinct sonic identities, such as natural ambience, destructive machinery, promotional fantasy, or grounded footsteps.
- Prefer intentional, high-quality music and SFX over generic filler when the project needs a premium documentary feel.
- Prefer licensed, platform-safe, or cleared music; do not treat downloader access as permission to use a track.
- Audition several plausible tracks for important music-led sections and choose by story tone, not recognizability.
- Use EQ, gain, stereo width, and room texture to make diegetic music bridges feel sourced before they open into full mix.
- Beauty-over-horror contrast must sharpen discomfort or critique rather than make harm feel stylish.
- Keep the full mix out of red/clipping and leave practical headroom before export.
- Music should fit the speaker's pace and scene tone; tighten the cut if music is only hiding slowness.
- Use short fades on SFX and music edges to avoid clicks.
- Start whooshes around -10 dB below dialogue in dense short-form edits, then adjust by ear.
- Keep low background music under speech unless the section is intentionally music-led.
- Start from whoosh, riser, and impact families, then add context-specific sounds only when the visual beat needs them.
- Use footsteps, cloth, object handling, and room texture to ground human cutaways only when a visible action supports the cue.
- A contrast drop should follow a clear build and re-enter dialogue cleanly.
- Use restrained piano/classical/editorial music beds for infographic explainers when they support narration and do not overdramatize data.
- Trim music to natural phrase endings and crossfade exits when possible.
- Use low-pass/EQ to make off-screen, outside-room, or behind-door sound feel
  farther away; use reverb or bus effects only when the space supports it.
- Use gentle dialogue compression for intelligibility, with modest gain reduction
  and makeup gain; stop before noise, breaths, or room tone become distracting.
- Remove any intro SFX cue that does not correspond to a visible event.
- Vary repeated intro hits, pops, and ticks so the opener does not sound like a pasted template.
- Under speech, a very low CapCut music value can be a starting point, but the final choice is intelligibility on phone speakers.
- If a music restore is tied to a visual filter reveal, the music rise should not
  cover the last word of the phrase.
- Treat downloaded SFX, songs, and character voice clips as temp until license or platform safety is documented.
- Fade SFX tails before the next clip or voice line if they mask the music or story beat.
- Copying a cue to a repeated visual event is acceptable only after checking that the second event has the same job and timing.
- Treat CapCut/app-library audio as needing the same license/platform status
  check as any external music or SFX before delivery.
- Text highlight, glow, and staggered reveal cues should be subtle enough that the
  viewer notices the text change, not the sound library.
- Clone and split-mask effects can use footsteps, door sounds, cloth, or room tone
  to sell space, but only if the sound matches visible or strongly implied action.
- Archival and filmed-screen sections can use light projector, room, or screen
  texture only when it supports source type and stays below speech.

## Caption Rules

- Subtitles should be added for hard-to-hear story-critical lines.
- Subtitles do not fully solve terrible audio; the line still needs to be understandable.
- Caption timing must match the repaired or edited audio exactly.
- Avoid captions during sensory pause beats unless accessibility or comprehension requires non-speech sound description.
- Captions can support music-heavy dialogue, but they do not excuse a mix that buries speech.
- Do not cover the visual turn where a music cue or drop lands.
- Do not caption subtle foley unless the non-speech sound is meaningful for accessibility.

## Color Rules

- No direct color rule, but busy visuals or high contrast should not make audio-repair captions hard to read.
- Music may motivate a more stylized grade, but color must still preserve faces, action, and evidence.
- Do not combine beauty-over-horror music with a grade that glamorizes damage or hides context.

## Tool Implementation Notes

- For ffmpeg, use loudness checks, waveform review, volume automation, highpass/lowpass, and denoise filters where appropriate.
- Use sidechain compression or manual ducking under dialogue.
- For Remotion, use audio gain envelopes and subtitle overlays for hard-to-hear lines.
- For Premiere, use Essential Sound cleanup, manual keyframes, captions, and clear project notes for any ADR/dub.
- For CapCut, use noise reduction, manual volume keyframes, and burned captions for key lines.
- For character arcs, audition different tracks against the same scene and choose the one that communicates the correct character state.
- For sensory worldbuilding, label each sound cue by story job: place, material, destruction, fantasy, proof, pause, or grounded reset.
- In ffmpeg or NLE mixes, check waveform peaks around pause beats and VO re-entry so SFX do not mask the next line.
- For track hygiene, group dialogue, music, SFX, and ambience separately; check peaks after all effects are added.
- For SFX libraries, tag sounds by job: whoosh, hit, pop, riser, ambience, meme, texture, or transition.
- For short-form motion sync, mark text pop, icon pop, zoom, reveal, reverse-hit start, impact frame, and SFX tail.
- For music auditions, duplicate the same section, align each candidate track's strongest cue to the same story beat, render short tests, and record reject reasons.
- For diegetic bridges, automate EQ, gain, stereo width, and filter release rather than hard-switching from source sound to full mix.
- Track license status before locking the edit.
- For social tracks, record whether the audio is licensed, platform-safe, temp-only, or unsafe before export.
- For Premiere finishing after AE, keep reusable SFX bins by job and place cues against the imported animation render with clip gain and short fades.
- For isolated SFX passes, mark whether dialogue/music were restored, SFX were lowered, and fades were applied.
- For slow human shorts, mark foley cue frames, micro-drop duration, build/drop markers, and dialogue re-entry gain.
- For infographic sequences, mark title, route, marker, chart, highlight, screenshot swap, final collage, and music exit cue frames.
- For Fairlight mixes, document production channel mapping, track names/colors,
  bus routing, ambience bed, music splice points, SFX passes, EQ distance effects,
  and dialogue dynamics settings.
- For intro mixes, store music BPM, eventFrame, cueType, gain, fadeOutFrames, proofQuoteRange, and bodyStartFrame.
- For CapCut-style music ducking, store musicFullLevel, duckStartFrame, lowLevel, restoreFrame, speechRange, and licenseStatus.
- For music-linked filter reveals, store speechClearFrame, musicRestoreFrame,
  filterStartStrength, filterEndStrength, and fullMixReview status.
- For anime/action SFX spotting, store cueFrame, cueJob, sourceStatus, voiceLineStartFrame, tailFadeFrames, and fullMixReview status.
- For CapCut, import local cues through Sounds > Device > Select from device, then align and trim them against the visual hit in the full mix.
- For CapCut pro effects, document sourceStatus for music, SFX, sticker sounds,
  and green-screen effect audio; align cues to subject-sandwich reveal, chroma
  portal zoom, sticker pop, VFX impact, or clone action frames.
- For viral text effects, store textEffectType, flickerFrames or changeFrames,
  tickCueFrame, cueEndFrame, gain, and fullMixReview status.
- For premium CapCut text reveals, store revealStartFrame, revealEndFrame,
  cueType, cueEndFrame, gain, and fullMixReview status.
- For documentary research overlays, store riserStartFrame, discoveryFrame,
  riserFadeFrame, narrationRange, and source/license status.

## Common Mistakes

- Letting music or SFX bury testimony.
- Assuming subtitles solve every bad-audio problem.
- Using cheerful or cliched stock music over serious documentary material.
- Adding isolated sync sound to one B-roll shot while the rest of the sequence is unnaturally silent.
- Failing to plan the music exit from a needle-drop moment.
- Using the same music tone through confidence, failure, and growth.
- Choosing music only because it sounds cool.
- Filling a strong pause beat with VO because silence feels risky.
- Adding generic cinematic SFX that do not describe the actual story world.
- Cutting to silence without a grounded image or natural sound to receive it.
- Choosing a track because it is famous instead of because it improves the story.
- Keeping a fascinating music experiment that is ethically or tonally wrong.
- Letting a music cue land on a meaningless shot.
- Using captions to compensate for music that should have been ducked.
- Mixing all audio on one track and losing control late in the edit.
- Using the same whoosh on every movement.
- Letting SFX hit red because the moment feels more exciting.
- Using upbeat music to avoid tightening a slow section.
- Using whooshes for every movement until speech feels buried.
- Placing reversed hits before beats that do not reveal anything.
- Leaving background music loud enough that subtitles become necessary for normal speech.
- Choosing a trendy track that fights the speaker's tone or makes the edit feel emotionally wrong.
- Adding SFX before animation timing is locked and leaving cues late or early.
- Using uncleared downloaded music instead of licensed or platform-safe sources.
- Forgetting to unmute required dialogue, music, or SFX tracks before export.
- Leaving SFX levels too hot because they sounded exciting while soloed.
- Adding early music before the voiceover cut is understandable.
- Making tiny foley loud enough that it feels fake.
- Dropping to silence or black without enough build to make the reset intentional.
- Adding SFX to every small infographic layer until the explanation feels cheap.
- Cutting explainer music abruptly instead of using a natural phrase or crossfade.
- Leaving production WAV dialogue on one speaker because the channel format was not mapped to mono tracks.
- Routing related SFX to a bus but forgetting to route that bus to the main output.
- Overcompressing dialogue until it sounds louder but less natural.
- Adding whooshes or hits because the intro feels expensive, not because the screen event needs sound.
- Letting intro music or impacts bury the proof quote that was supposed to build trust.
- Starting a music duck after the speaker has already begun talking.
- Restoring the music under the end of a sentence because the montage resumes visually.
- Copying a -15 dB or -20 dB duck to every song without checking the real voice
  and music balance.
- Raising a filter and music together before the spoken phrase has finished.
- Placing anime/action voice lines on every clip instead of only identity or impact beats.
- Judging intense SFX while soloed and skipping the full music/dialogue review.
- Assuming a downloaded character voice, song, or SFX is cleared for publishing.
- Reusing one dramatic hit for unrelated visual jobs such as eye transforms, lightning, glass overlays, and entrances.
- Assuming CapCut library audio, screen-recorded green-screen effect audio, or
  downloaded SFX is safe because it was easy to import.
- Adding a dramatic riser to ordinary browsing or research footage with no actual
  payoff.
- Letting archival projector, screen, or texture sounds distract from narration
  or source context.
- Leaving ticking SFX under the next sentence after a text flicker or font shift
  has ended.
- Letting a glitch or shimmer cue continue after a highlight wipe, staggered
  reveal, or glow sweep has finished.

## QC Checklist

- Every story-critical line is intelligible to a first-time viewer.
- Hard-to-hear lines are repaired, subtitled, or cut.
- Music rights are safe for the intended release.
- Music tone respects the subject matter.
- SFX and music duck under important dialogue.
- Ambience is consistent across related B-roll sequences.
- No clipping, harsh pops, or painful transient spikes.
- The music communicates the intended character state and story phase.
- Every sensory SFX cue has a named story job.
- Pause beats feel intentional and not like dead air.
- Abrupt silence resets land after a ramp and do not create a technical-sounding audio dropout.
- Important music-led sections have an intended tone and at least one alternate audition.
- Licensed music status is documented or the track is marked temp-only.
- Needle-drop cues land on meaningful visual or story beats.
- Diegetic music bridges have a plausible source and clear full-mix transition.
- Sensitive music contrast creates critique, grief, or discomfort rather than glamour.
- Dialogue, music, SFX, and ambience are controllable as separate tracks or layers.
- Quiet story-critical words are repaired without causing clipped peaks.
- Repeated SFX use variation rather than the exact same file every time.
- SFX hits land on the intended motion or impact frame.
- Music improves tone and pacing instead of masking an edit that should be cut tighter.
- Whooshes start low enough that speech remains dominant.
- Reversed hits build into real questions, reveals, or transitions.
- Background music is low enough that a first-time viewer can follow speech without captions.
- Social music choice is documented by intended tone, reject reason, and license/platform status.
- SFX cues are synced to final visible AE motion, not an earlier rough-cut placeholder.
- Music is split or ducked where dialogue needs space.
- All required audio tracks are unmuted and checked in the final export range.
- After isolated SFX spotting, dialogue and music are restored and speech remains clear.
- Early music choices are rechecked after visuals, SFX, and color are complete.
- Human cutaway foley matches visible movement, material, and surface.
- Contrast drops re-enter cleanly and do not sound like technical dropouts.
- Infographic SFX cue only meaningful visible events and remain below narration.
- Editorial music beds are licensed/platform-safe and do not overdramatize maps, data, or evidence.
- Fairlight dialogue, ambience, music, SFX, bus routing, EQ, and dynamics have
  been checked in the full mix and final export range.
- Every intro SFX cue lands on a visible event and repeated cues vary enough to avoid a mechanical template feel.
- Proof quotes and the first body line remain intelligible under the intro music bed.
- Intro music resolves, ducks, or fades cleanly into the main content.
- Music beds duck before speech, restore after speech clears, and remain licensed/platform-safe or temp-marked.
- Music-duck values are checked against the actual track and voice, not copied
  blindly from a preset.
- Music-linked filter reveals keep the last spoken words intelligible and the
  full mix unclipped.
- Anime/action SFX cues land on visible motion, impact, reveal, transition, or identity moments.
- Cue families match the visible event, and repeated cues are justified by repeated visuals rather than habit.
- Voice lines improve clarity or intensity without cluttering every clip.
- SFX and voice-line sources are licensed/platform-safe or marked temp-only.
- The full mix preserves music, dialogue, SFX, and voice lines without clipping.
- CapCut pro-effect cues land on visible events and every imported/app-library
  audio asset is licensed, platform-safe, or marked temp-only.
- Viral text ticks/clicks land on visible text changes, end with the animation,
  and stay below dialogue.
- Premium text glitch/shimmer cues land on the visible reveal, end with the effect,
  and stay below dialogue.
- Research risers, archival texture, and filmed-screen ambience resolve cleanly
  and stay below narration.
