# Editing Taste: Story And Pacing

## Purpose

Help the agent choose what belongs in the edit, what should be removed, and how the
timeline should feel.

## When To Use This Skill

- Building the first cut.
- Trimming for clarity, emotion, rhythm, or story momentum.
- Planning a montage, action sequence, documentary opening, or long-form story section.
- Deciding whether a moment needs air, compression, silence, contrast, or removal.
- Building the rough cut and deciding what polish can wait until the structure works.
- Balancing artful expression, audience clarity, and subject sensitivity.
- Building a protagonist-driven challenge, learning arc, or transformation story.
- Designing long-form invitation intros that build curiosity, world, and trust before the explicit promise.
- Judging whether music changes the story in a useful or harmful direction.
- Judging whether a short-form hook, claim, visual stack, or rest beat supports the actual idea.
- Choosing when a slower human short should preserve imperfection, proof, warmth, or breathing room.
- Deciding when a short-form edit is good enough as a simple cut versus when it needs Resolve/Fusion polish.
- Choosing which Premiere short-form body visual belongs on a line: none, filler card, B-roll text, or full-screen animation.
- Planning an AE-heavy Reel so every animated segment supports the message instead of merely looking advanced.
- Checking whether a viral-style YouTube idea, script, secondary story, and publish threshold are ready before polish.
- Judging whether a branded intro bumper strengthens trust and story packaging or only delays the promised value.

## Core Principles

- Preserve moments that advance story, reveal character, prove a claim, or create an emotional turn.
- Cut repetition unless repetition is the point.
- Prefer clear cause-and-effect over decorative montage.
- Watch eyes, faces, breath, body language, and silence for decisions or emotional turns.
- Do not accept vague labels like "montage" as a plan; write beat intent before selecting clips.
- In action scenes, identify whose experience or stakes drive the sequence before emphasizing spectacle.
- Match edit tone to the subject. Serious material needs empathy and confidence more than flashy speed.
- Define whether the audience is broad, niche, short-form, long-form, or art-first before judging pace.
- Do not confuse editor effort with viewer value.
- Editing is downstream from the idea, title, thumbnail, promise, and story value.
- Build a clean rough cut before polishing effects, captions, SFX, or color.
- Editing should supplement the value rather than become the star of a weak premise.
- Match supporting visuals to the exact spoken point instead of using B-roll as filler.
- Short-form idea quality comes before polish; trends and AI prompts need niche specificity and a supported claim.
- High-energy hooks should lead into readable explanation instead of exhausting the viewer.
- Show the spoken idea visually when a text, icon, stock shot, or proof frame communicates faster than narration.
- A reusable preset workflow is an editorial advantage only when the underlying effects have clear story jobs.
- Efficiency and consistency matter in short-form work; more hours and more effects do not guarantee stronger retention.
- The visual support type should match the importance of the line, not the editor's desire to use a flashier effect.
- A polished Reel workflow works best in phases: clean cut, captions, hook, body support, post motion, music.
- A consistent editing signature can help a creator or client feel recognizable, but it must remain flexible enough to serve the current message.
- Visual planning should happen at the segment level: decide what each cut needs to show before opening a complex animation tool.
- Viral-style editing starts before the timeline: target audience, idea strength, and script structure decide whether polish has something worth amplifying.
- A secondary story can make a tutorial or explainer feel human, but only when it supports the main promise and progress.
- Preserve pauses when they create tension, humor, emotion, or comprehension; speed is not a reason to cut meaningful space.
- Preserve imperfect human texture when it creates trust, warmth, vulnerability, or character.
- Slow short-form pacing still needs a clear first-seconds reason to watch.
- Publish decisions should separate blocking issues from optional improvements.
- Every major story beat should answer what the situation means to the protagonist.
- Conflict is stronger when the edit shows how the protagonist's belief, confidence, or status is challenged.
- Expert, mentor, or guide characters need proof of credibility before their feedback carries weight.
- If the source footage lacks a necessary perspective beat, consider a truthful pickup rather than forcing weak footage.
- Long-form intros can invite instead of hijack: open specific question loops, close one early, then build world and conflict before the full promise.
- Worldbuilding is strongest when the place, material, institution, or fantasy is treated like a character with its own texture and conflict.
- Music choice can change the perceived genre, quality, ethics, and emotional meaning of the same footage.
- The editor should be willing to reject a clever music experiment when the storyteller's final meaning is wrong.
- A wide or anchor shot can give an action sequence structure; closeups and
  inserts should clarify action, perspective, or consequence instead of replacing geography.
- A formal intro is story packaging, not decoration: it should quickly establish brand/show identity, external proof or authority, and viewer outcome when those beats matter.

Applicability note: fast retention editing can be right for short-form feed work, but sensitive long-form documentary should often slow down after the viewer understands why the video matters.
Contradiction note: earlier hook rules prefer confirming the promise quickly. Prefer that rule for short-form/feed-first work. For high-trust long-form documentary, a delayed promise can be stronger if the intro creates specific questions, closes at least one early loop, and actively builds world, conflict, or trust.
Contradiction note: formal bumpers can increase perceived value for podcasts, shows, courses, creator brands, communities, or social series. Prefer immediate content on ordinary mid-length YouTube videos unless the bumper creates trust faster than the body would.

## Timing Rules

- A few seconds of hold time can change the emotion of a shot; test alternate versions for important beats.
- For sensitive grief, danger, destruction, or testimony, test a 2-5 second reaction or observation hold before cutting away.
- In a build/peak/release sequence, post-peak release may need to be as long as or longer than the buildup.
- For invisible rhythm, cut on a readable cue: glance, breath, completed gesture, step, line ending, or environmental cycle.
- For intentional discomfort, hold slightly past expected rhythm only when the scene justifies it.
- Long-form observational shots often need 1.5-3 seconds or more when the viewer must scan dense information.
- Hold maps and route graphics until the viewer understands the route or distance.
- In challenge or learning videos, establish overconfidence or perceived ability before the humbling obstacle.
- Expert proof should be short and sharp, often 2-3 examples before the meeting or feedback beat.
- Pickup object/detail inserts often need 1-3 seconds depending on visual density.
- Repetition for scale usually reads with 2-4 examples; stop when the pattern is clear.
- In long-form invitation intros, close one early question loop before stacking more mystery.
- A 1-2 second pause beat can let image and sound deliver meaning without extra VO.
- Fast cutting can express scale or overwhelm when the shots share clear visual cues; slow down afterward for the reveal or proof.
- A music cue should land on a story turn, action change, emotional hit, or section reset.
- Remove accidental dead air in talking-head and short-form edits unless the pause has tension, humor, emotion, or comprehension value.
- Front-load polish into the first 30 seconds and first minute when early drop-off risk is high.
- Use J/L cuts or short audio crossfades when removing pauses would otherwise make speech feel harsh.
- Do not reveal the full payoff so early that the viewer loses the reason to continue.
- In feed-first shorts, the first five seconds should carry the main claim, question, desire, proof, or curiosity gap.
- In slower feed-first shorts, use the first 3-5 seconds for motion, proof, face, or another clear retention reason before asking for patience.
- Short-form black/drop gaps should usually stay below one second unless a longer cinematic pause has a named purpose.
- After a dense hook, add a readable rest beat if the viewer needs time to process the idea.
- Build simple vertical A-roll/B-roll/captions before spending time on Fusion polish.
- In a Premiere Reel workflow, complete silence removal and J-cut smoothing before judging caption, hook, or motion polish.
- Hook text design should earn attention quickly, then the body should move through lighter or heavier visuals based on line value.
- Segment ideation should be completed before AE animation so the editor can choose simple captions, speaker plus text, B-roll, icons, full-screen scenes, or path/counter work by story need.
- Structure scripted videos so the main lesson and any secondary story both progress from start to finish.
- Remove useless lines, weak jokes, and repeated points, but keep pauses that make the stop worth waiting for.
- Cut on action when an insert or closeup should hide a continuity mismatch while
  preserving believable motion.
- Formal intros should be capped tightly: about 3-5 seconds for social series and about 10-15 seconds maximum for podcast/show or long-form brand packaging.
- If a bumper delays the body, it must earn that delay with brand identity, credible proof, or viewer outcome rather than logo time.

## Motion Rules

- Keep added motion secondary when faces, grief, testimony, or evidence carry the scene.
- For smooth B-roll strings, prefer consistent movement direction and clear focal points.
- Use wide-to-close contrast when a sequence needs both epic scale and human detail.
- Avoid trendy wipes, goofy transitions, or old channel templates when they clash with mature subject matter.
- Use match cuts only when the visual match adds meaning.
- Keep small-object demonstrations or hand actions center-frame unless off-center framing has a story purpose.
- Use pickups or inserts to reveal story context, not to decorate a scene.
- Use treatment grammar, such as letterbox, subtle rotation, or movement direction, only when it separates story worlds or perspectives.
- Tracking back can pull the viewer out of a fantasy or reveal scale; tracking forward can invite the viewer into a world.
- Use punch-ins, slides, screenshots, overlays, or B-roll to hide jump cuts only when they also clarify the current point.
- Direct the viewer's eye to one primary focus; if the important action is small, crop, zoom, arrow, or reframe it.
- Keep faces or proof artifacts aligned across early hook cuts so slower human openings still feel smooth.
- A long simple dynamic zoom can carry an emotional section when the story already has enough human pull.
- Use visual stacks to show abstract ideas, but keep hierarchy clear between text, icons, stock footage, captions, and background.
- B-roll should appear where the line needs proof, status, mood, or contrast, not merely because the timeline feels plain.
- Use filler cards when text alone carries the phrase; use B-roll when the line needs experience or proof; use full-screen animation for high-value abstract ideas.
- Add adjustment zooms only after the story support is already clear.
- Keep branded background, icon, and text choices consistent enough that the Reel feels designed, not randomly collected.
- Match the motion style to the audience and creator voice before copying a popular editing style from another niche.
- Use closeups or pickup inserts over an anchor shot only where they answer what
  the viewer should see, feel, or understand next.
- Premium intro motion should preserve one story focus at a time; brand, proof, and outcome should not compete in the same frame.

## Sound Rules

- Silence, ambience, and music can carry meaning; long-form does not need dialogue at all times.
- For serious material, restrained music and real ambience often serve better than upbeat stock tracks.
- Preserve breaths, room tone, and emotional quiet when they support the beat.
- Music and SFX must not overpower story-critical dialogue or testimony.
- Music can define character state: overconfidence, humbling, learning, or earned confidence.
- Sound can characterize a place, material, or unreal world before the VO explains it.
- Abrupt silence works best after a ramped sound-image sequence and should return to grounded natural sound.
- Audition music by story fit and tone, not just energy, recognizability, or personal taste.
- Beauty-over-horror music needs an ethical reason and a restrained comparison pass.
- Music should match the scene pace and tone; do not use upbeat music as a bandage for slow cutting.
- Opening breaths or inhales should be kept only when they set the intended human tone.
- Repeated SFX should vary enough that the edit does not sound copied and pasted.
- Use subtle realistic foley to make human cutaways believable when the physical movement needs texture.
- Build contrast with sound and cuts only when a drop, open-space beat, or calmer section receives the release.
- Reverse hits and whooshes should support real questions, reveals, and movement rather than filling every gap.
- Music should be chosen after the body edit is visible enough to judge the actual tone.
- For high-quality Reels, choose final music after the animation is visible enough to judge tone, pace, and cue alignment.
- Music should support the script's emotional and pacing plan instead of deciding the story for it.
- Action inserts need sound or ambience continuity so the sequence feels like one
  event rather than separate unrelated shots.
- Intro SFX and music should increase perceived quality only when they land on visible events and leave proof or body dialogue clear.

## Caption Rules

- Captions should clarify story, not compensate for unclear editing.
- Subtitles for hard-to-hear story lines take priority over lower thirds or decorative labels.
- Captions should not cover eyes, faces, maps, evidence, or action that carries the beat.
- Captions should not cover small-object actions or reaction shots that carry protagonist perspective.
- In sensory pause beats, avoid captions unless they are needed for comprehension.
- On-screen text should not stack title, card, captions, and explanation so heavily that the viewer loses the main point.
- Captions and overlays need enough contrast, stroke, shadow, or backing to stay readable at delivery size.
- Captions should remain visible, repositioned, or simplified when visual text already carries the idea. Suppress only with explicit user approval.
- Text+ premium captions should emphasize key ideas, not replace all subtitle work.
- Use center/emphasis text only for important ideas that captions cannot carry cleanly.
- In warm human shorts, captions should be readable but restrained enough that faces, proof, and reactions remain primary.
- In designed intros, keep duplicate captions visible or repositioned when brand, proof, or outcome text already communicates the words. Suppress only with explicit user approval.

## Color Rules

- Preserve faces, eye detail, damage, evidence, and readable geography.
- Avoid glossy travel-vlog looks for war, crisis, grief, or other serious documentary material unless the contrast is intentional.
- Match B-roll color enough that a sequence feels intentionally assembled.
- If color or aspect treatment separates reality from fantasy, apply the grammar consistently and preserve evidence readability.
- Warm hopeful grades should preserve skin, proof, and caption contrast before adding glow, grain, radial blur, or vignette.

## Tool Implementation Notes

- For ffmpeg workflows, render short A/B timing tests around emotional beats and inspect contact sheets around cut points.
- Store montage and documentary beats as EDL, JSON, markers, or timeline placeholders before rendering.
- Use contact sheets to identify repeated shots, weak focal points, and shots cut too quickly to scan.
- For Remotion workflows, represent holds, beats, focal points, and source timestamps as explicit data.
- For CapCut or Premiere equivalents, use markers, labels, duplicate sequences, and manual trims instead of default effect templates.
- For documentary reordering, keep original source timestamps and flag sensitive chronology changes for review.
- For challenge/learning arcs, tag beats by phase: overconfidence, humbling, learning, competence.
- For pickups, compare adjacent shots for color, texture, sound bed, and continuity.
- For delayed intros, track open loops by ID and mark their closure, proof, or reframing beats.
- For visual-treatment sequences, mark clips as reality, fantasy, proof, or exception before applying effects.
- For music-led story decisions, keep candidate track notes with intended tone, cue point, license status, and reject reason.
- For rough-cut workflows, keep pass markers such as rough, visual support, polish, audio, and export QC.
- For slow human shorts, compare original, shorter, and balanced timeline versions before locking polish.
- For human interruptions, record the connection purpose and any consent, privacy, or dignity risk before keeping the beat.
- For AI-generated clips or layouts, treat tool output as a draft and keep approval notes for context, start/end trims, captions, and layout.
- For short-form hooks, store claim type, proof/payoff, trend note, and whether the claim is supported.
- For visual stacks, label each layer by the spoken concept it explains.
- For Resolve preset systems, name each reusable effect by its story job and visual behavior.
- For AE-heavy Reels, keep an ideation board with spoken segment, intended viewer focus, visual support type, assets needed, caption continuity policy, and any user-approved suppression exception.
- For viral-style scripts, track audience, idea gate, main promise, secondary story beats, optional improvements, and publish blockers separately.
- For action sequences, mark the anchor shot, action overlap point, insert reason,
  and return/continuation point before polishing.
- For formal intro review, mark introAllowed, format, brand/proof/outcome beats, duration cap, and bodyStartFrame before judging polish.

## Common Mistakes

- Cutting away before the viewer can read the emotional turn.
- Treating silence as filler.
- Cutting B-roll only because a talking head has been onscreen for a while.
- Scattering related shots instead of grouping them into story beats.
- Using fast retention habits throughout long-form documentary.
- Letting spectacle replace character stakes.
- Making clever transitions that distract from meaning.
- Reordering documentary material in a way that changes factual meaning or speaker intent.
- Showing impressive visuals without showing what they mean to the protagonist.
- Closing the promised goal too early and draining curiosity.
- Introducing an expert through labels before showing why they matter.
- Filming pickups that fabricate instead of clarifying truth.
- Delaying the promise without creating trust, conflict, or curiosity.
- Opening mystery loops that never close.
- Using letterbox, rotation, or movement changes as style rather than story grammar.
- Letting music make a serious story feel stylish at the expense of dignity.
- Keeping a famous track that makes the scene worse because it feels exciting in isolation.
- Polishing effects before the story cut is understandable.
- Using B-roll only because the talking head has been onscreen for a while.
- Starting with mechanism, product, or jargon before the viewer's desired outcome is clear.
- Leaving accidental opening silence, breath, or hesitation that sets the wrong tone.
- Mistaking a busy timeline for a stronger viewer experience.
- Using a viral-sounding claim that the edit cannot pay off.
- Keeping maximum visual intensity after the hook until the explanation becomes hard to follow.
- Showing every word as text instead of choosing the fastest visual representation.
- Confusing Power Bin speed with editorial judgment.
- Polishing random B-roll instead of choosing B-roll that supports the line.
- Spending full-screen animation effort on low-value body lines.
- Choosing body visuals by fixed schedule instead of story importance.
- Using music trendiness to avoid deciding what the scene should feel like.
- Building complex animation for low-value lines that only needed a clean cut or simple text.
- Mixing fonts, icons, colors, and backgrounds that do not belong to the same editing signature.
- Starting polish before the rough cut, segment purpose, and visual support plan are stable.
- Adding a secondary story that delays the main promise without increasing tension, emotion, or payoff.
- Treating every possible improvement as a reason not to publish.
- Treating "slow" as permission to leave unshaped dead air.
- Cutting the human imperfection that made the story feel trustworthy.
- Adding hyper-edit effects to a long section that only needed proof, warmth, or a simple zoom.
- Cutting into closeups without an anchor shot or clear action overlap, causing
  the viewer to lose the scene geography.
- Keeping a formal intro because it looks premium even though it weakens story momentum or delays the promised value.
- Using self-praise where external proof or audience/community evidence would build more trust.

## QC Checklist

- The viewer understands the premise and subject stakes early.
- Each section has a reason to exist.
- Emotional turns are visible without extra explanation.
- Montage sections have beat-level intent.
- Every B-roll shot serves a story point, contrast, scale cue, detail, or consequence.
- Action stays anchored to character perspective and remains readable.
- Natural rhythm cuts feel invisible when watched cold.
- Jarring cuts have a clear emotional purpose.
- Match cuts create only intended meanings.
- Sensitive documentary edits preserve subject dignity and truth.
- The protagonist's goal, obstacle, learning path, and payoff are visible.
- Expert or mentor introductions include credible proof before influence.
- Pickup inserts solve a specific story gap and preserve truth.
- Invitation intros close an early loop before asking for more patience.
- Delayed promises are justified by worldbuilding, proof, contrast, or protagonist perspective.
- Reality/fantasy visual grammar has clear meaning and no accidental exceptions.
- Music choices support the story stance and do not merely decorate the footage.
- Sensitive music contrasts sharpen meaning without glamourizing harm.
- The first 30 seconds match the title, thumbnail, and promised viewer outcome.
- Any removed pause is truly dead air, not a meaningful beat.
- Each B-roll, slide, screenshot, arrow, or overlay matches the spoken point it supports.
- The edit still provides clear value if decorative effects are temporarily disabled.
- The opening shows enough proof or tension without fully spoiling the payoff.
- The hook claim is supported by a later payoff or proof beat.
- Any post-hook rest beat keeps momentum and improves comprehension.
- Visual stack elements clarify the idea and do not merely decorate it.
- Resolve presets are reusable because they clarify repeated needs, not because they are flashy.
- The simple cut would still make sense before Fusion effects are added.
- The Reel still has a clear story path before post zooms and music are enabled.
- Each body visual type is justified by the spoken line's importance.
- The music choice supports the final body edit's tone rather than changing it accidentally.
- Every AE-heavy segment has a clear message job and justified complexity level.
- The Reel's editing signature is coherent without making every segment feel identical.
- Final music and visual style support the body edit instead of changing the story by accident.
- Target audience, idea promise, and style lane are written before polish.
- The secondary story, if present, supports the main video instead of competing with it.
- Remaining improvements are optional after blocking story, audio, caption, rights, and export issues are cleared.
- Slow short-form sections have named story, proof, emotion, or comprehension purposes.
- Kept imperfections build connection and create no consent, privacy, or dignity risk.
- Warm human grades and captions preserve faces, proof, and readability.
- Action inserts preserve spatial clarity, cut on readable movement, and answer a
  story or perspective question.
- Formal intros pass the format gate and communicate brand, credible proof, and viewer outcome before the body starts.
- If the bumper is removed, the edit becomes weaker because trust/package value is lost, not clearer because delay is gone.
