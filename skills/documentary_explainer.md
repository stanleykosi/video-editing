# Documentary Explainer

## Purpose

Guide narrative explainers, mini-documentaries, evidence-led edits, archival flow,
and clear visual teaching.

## When To Use This Skill

- Editing explainers, mini-docs, educational videos, travel documentaries, or evidence-based narratives.
- Combining voiceover, footage, interviews, screenshots, diagrams, maps, and citations.
- Turning vlog or montage material into a structured documentary-style sequence.
- Working with sensitive subjects where tone, truth, and empathy matter.
- Building a documentary, essay, or challenge around a protagonist's perspective.
- Building an invitation intro where world, proof, tone, and trust arrive before the full promise.
- Using music to shape documentary tone, genre read, or sensitive contrast.
- Turning tutorial, screen-recording, or educational footage into clear visual proof and explanation.
- Building editorial infographic sequences with maps, charts, source documents, screenshots, and textured graphic backgrounds.
- Choosing documentary/explainer typography that improves evidence hierarchy without copying a publisher identity.
- Building CapCut documentary graphics with cutout depth, switch-focus blur,
  research overlays, archival treatments, filmed-screen texture, and parallax
  collage scenes.

## Core Principles

- Build around a clear question, tension, thesis, or human stake.
- Use visuals as evidence, not wallpaper.
- Make transitions logical: cause, contrast, escalation, consequence, or relief.
- Lead serious subjects with the people, place, stakes, and evidence before centering the creator.
- Write montage sections as beat-by-beat arguments before cutting.
- Keep artful documentary texture subordinate to viewer comprehension and subject dignity.
- Reorder material only when truth, causality, speaker intent, and ethics survive the change.
- Use protagonist reaction and POV shots to show what evidence or events mean emotionally.
- When a story is about learning, show overconfidence, humbling, practice, and earned competence rather than only explaining them.
- A long-form documentary can worldbuild before the explicit promise when the world itself creates curiosity and the edit closes early trust loops.
- Ground polished documentary footage with human creator moments when the piece should still feel firsthand and YouTube-native.
- Proof shots, maps, screenshots, and construction/evidence frames can close major trust loops and should not be treated as filler.
- Music choice is a documentary argument: it can make footage feel intimate, premium, stylized, haunting, or disrespectful.
- Reject a compelling music experiment when it weakens truth, dignity, or the intended story stance.
- Match every screenshot, slide, B-roll shot, and graphic to the line or claim it supports.
- Explain mechanisms after the viewer understands the pain, desired result, or reason to care.
- Translate expert jargon into viewer outcomes using the pattern: teach X so that the viewer gets Y.
- Editorial graphic style is only useful when it makes evidence, geography, or data easier to understand.
- Do not copy another publisher's protected identity; adapt the documentary graphic grammar to the current project.
- Serif typography can add editorial or historical gravity, but evidence readability still comes first.
- Clean sans labels work well for utility, source notes, and screen-reading clarity.
- Typography should tell the viewer which claim, source word, map label, or data point matters first.
- Documentary depth effects work only when they clarify contrast, source type,
  investigation, or story hierarchy; they should not make evidence feel fake.
- AI-generated or downloaded documentary images are illustrative assets until
  source/license status and labeling make their role clear.

## Timing Rules

- Give complex ideas enough time to be understood.
- Hold maps, documents, route graphics, signs, and dense destruction until the viewer can read or scan them.
- Long-form audiences can handle slower observational sequences after the commitment point.
- For sensitive reactions, test a slower version before choosing fast compression.
- A title card can work after a strong long-form opening; in short videos it may waste scarce time.
- Hold protagonist reactions long enough for the viewer to understand the personal meaning of evidence or events.
- Close one early question loop in a delayed intro before adding more unanswered questions.
- Pause beats can last 1-2 seconds when sound and image show scale, conflict, or unreality better than VO.
- Proof barrages may be fast, but at least one proof beat must be readable enough to close the trust loop.
- Music-led documentary passages should be A/B tested against a restrained version when the subject is serious.
- Screenshots, slides, and proof frames should appear close enough to the spoken reference that they feel like evidence rather than decoration.
- Trim meandering educational setup unless it builds trust, context, or the viewer's desired result.
- Map route reveals, chart wipes, and document highlights should finish before the narration depends on the conclusion.
- Low-FPS/posterize texture belongs after timing lock and must not reduce evidence readability.
- Title, label, and source-text hierarchy should be readable before a map, chart, or document leaves screen.
- Switch-focus blur should leave at least one subject readable throughout the
  handoff.
- Documentary title animations around 1.5-2 seconds can feel polished when they
  settle before the word or source label must be read.
- Research timelapses can speed up process footage, but any proof or discovery
  frame must hold long enough to inspect.

## Motion Rules

- Use simple maps, restrained titles, and stable lower thirds for mature documentary tone.
- Use consistent movement direction in location-entry B-roll unless contrast is intentional.
- Use one strong establishing shot plus fewer, stronger focal shots instead of many weak quick cuts.
- Avoid screen wipes, trendy transitions, and comic graphics when they cheapen serious material.
- Keep small-object demonstrations, artifacts, screens, and pickup inserts readable.
- Use visual treatment grammar to separate grounded reality, promotional fantasy, proof, and perspective exceptions.
- Group tracking-back shots when pulling viewers out of a fantasy; group tracking-forward shots when inviting them into a world.
- Use arrows, zooms, dimming, or blur to direct attention on dense screens only when they make the claim easier to verify.
- Avoid loading the screen with title, card, caption, and screenshot text at the same time.
- Use paper texture, grid, routes, chart bars, highlight strips, and cutouts only when they support the current claim.
- Use compound/nested graphic scenes for maps, charts, documents, and screenshot focus so dense timelines remain editable.
- Place explanatory text in negative space or near the evidence it explains without covering the evidence.
- Foreground cutouts should move more strongly than backgrounds in parallax scenes;
  copied background transforms should be reduced so depth remains visible.
- Cross-blur focus handoffs should serve comparison or contrast, not hide weak
  source images.
- Filmed-screen texture should add camera realism while preserving UI and source text.

## Sound Rules

- Story-critical speech must be intelligible or subtitled.
- Use ambience and J-cuts to enter a location and maintain realism.
- If one B-roll shot has sync sound, apply a consistent ambience rule across the sequence.
- Music should respect the subject; avoid generic upbeat tracks over grief, war, or crisis.
- Music can mark shifts in protagonist confidence, failure, learning, or competence.
- Sound can characterize landscape, material, machinery, fantasy, or destruction before narration explains it.
- Abrupt silence after a ramped sequence should return the viewer to grounded natural sound, not empty dead air.
- Beautiful or nostalgic music over horrific imagery is allowed only when it creates critique, grief, or discomfort rather than spectacle.
- Licensed mainstream or recognizable tracks still need documentary tone review.
- Music should support the explanation's pace and tone, not disguise unclear structure.
- Use J/L cuts or ambience bridges when entering proof, B-roll, or screen-recording sections.
- Infographic SFX should land on meaningful reveals, highlights, route movement, or screenshot swaps and stay below narration.
- Documentary graphic music should support clarity and pace without overdramatizing data or evidence.
- Research-section risers should build to a visible discovery or claim and stay
  below narration.
- Subtle ambience, projector texture, or room tone can support archival sections
  only when it does not distract from source context.

## Caption Rules

- Subtitles for important testimony beat decorative lower thirds.
- Captions should not cover faces, evidence, maps, or key action.
- Use captions to preserve comprehension when audio is compromised.
- Do not cover object inserts or protagonist reactions needed for perspective.
- Do not cover maps, proof screenshots, body language, or creator-grounding moments that close trust loops.
- If music makes testimony harder to hear, fix the mix before relying on subtitles.
- Captions and callouts should not cover the evidence or UI detail being explained.
- When captions and screenshots both contain text, decide which one is primary before placing the other.
- Captions should move away from maps, chart labels, source documents, and highlighted screenshots.
- Serif/display title text should not compete with source-document text that viewers need to inspect.
- Captions should avoid cutout faces, blurred focus targets, research documents,
  archival source labels, and filmed-screen UI details.

## Color Rules

- Preserve faces, evidence, geography, and damage.
- Avoid overly glossy or poppy grades that make serious material feel like a travel promo.
- Match stock and original footage enough that the sequence feels coherent.
- If aspect ratio, letterbox, rotation, or color indicates reality versus fantasy, preserve that grammar consistently.
- Avoid grades that combine with beautiful music to glamorize harm or hide documentary evidence.
- Warm paper palettes should preserve map labels, chart values, and source text contrast.
- Typography accent colors should identify claims, categories, or contrast rather than implying unsupported urgency.
- Archival desaturation, particles, fade, and film frames should signal old media
  without hiding dates, faces, captions, or source text.
- Glass/screen textures and blend modes should be reduced when they lower proof
  readability.

## Tool Implementation Notes

- For ffmpeg, use contact sheets to review shot repetition, focal point clarity, maps, and hold times.
- Track documentary source timestamps in EDLs when reordering lines or events.
- For Remotion, store B-roll beat metadata: purpose, source, focal point, movement direction, duration, and audio rule.
- For Premiere, use bins for scale, detail, testimony, danger, normal life, maps, and contrast.
- For CapCut, remove trendy templates before judging the story; use restrained captions and clean cuts first.
- For perspective edits, label beats as event, protagonist reaction, emotional state, and consequence.
- For pickups, keep notes on what story gap each insert solves.
- For delayed intros, mark question loops, early closures, worldbuilding beats, proof beats, call to adventure, and promise delivery.
- For reality/fantasy sequences, assign treatment metadata before adding aspect bars, rotation, zoom, or color shifts.
- For sensitive music choices, store intended emotion, ethical risk, approval status, and reject reason.
- For educational explainers, mark each visual as proof, example, context, mechanism, outcome, or transition cover.
- For dense screen recordings, store a focus target and use keyframed crop/zoom or overlays only around that target.
- For editorial graphic sequences, store asset source/license notes for maps, screenshots, textures, cutouts, SFX, and music.
- For Remotion/Premiere/CapCut, model each graphic scene as intro, map, chart, document, screenshot focus, cutout collage, or transition.
- Store documentary typography tokens for title family, label family, source-text treatment, impact words, accent colors, and caption-safe regions.
- For CapCut documentary effects, store foreground cutout sources, mask feather,
  focus/blur keyframes, parallax target positions, background motion multiplier,
  archival treatment values, filmed-screen texture opacity, and rights labels.

## Common Mistakes

- Treating documentary B-roll as filler.
- Opening a serious piece like an apology video or creator stunt.
- Rushing dense shots before the viewer can understand them.
- Using graphics that clash with subject tone.
- Repeating shots because the schedule is rushed.
- Reordering events to create drama while changing factual meaning.
- Letting narration explain perspective that could be shown through reaction or POV.
- Adding pickups that look good but do not solve a story gap.
- Delaying the story promise while showing pretty footage that does not build conflict or trust.
- Using promotional or fantasy footage without a grounded reality counterpoint.
- Applying letterbox or cinematic treatment in a way that confuses proof with fantasy.
- Keeping a music choice because it is cinematic even though it misreads the subject.
- Using beauty-over-horror contrast without testing a restrained alternative.
- Showing B-roll or screenshots that do not match the current claim.
- Starting with tool mechanics before the viewer understands the outcome they want.
- Using expert jargon before defining the practical viewer result.
- Covering the exact proof point with captions or graphics.
- Copying a publisher's logo or exact graphic identity instead of using a project-specific editorial style.
- Adding low-FPS texture until maps, charts, or source documents become hard to read.
- Animating charts without known values or source context.
- Using serif or display typography as a style layer while the actual source evidence becomes harder to read.
- Covering source context with captions, titles, or oversized labels.
- Treating generated images, stock cutouts, or texture overlays as documentary
  evidence without labeling, source notes, or rights status.
- Applying film frames or archival particles to current proof and confusing the
  source type.
- Moving foreground and background at identical strength so a parallax scene reads
  as a flat slide.

## QC Checklist

- The viewer can follow the argument without prior context.
- Claims are supported by visible, audible, or cited evidence.
- Montage beats read like a structured argument, not a pile of attractive shots.
- The first minute respects the subject and confirms the video promise.
- Maps/routes are readable before they disappear.
- Sensitive reorders preserve truth and are flagged for review.
- Audio, captions, graphics, music, and color all match the documentary tone.
- Each major evidence beat connects back to protagonist perspective or the central argument.
- Pickups preserve truth and continuity.
- Delayed intros close at least one early loop and eventually deliver the promised story clearly.
- Maps, proof barrages, and screenshots are readable enough to build trust.
- Visual-treatment exceptions are tied to perspective, belief, or story logic.
- Sensitive music choices preserve dignity and do not turn harm into spectacle.
- Music-led sections keep narration, testimony, and context intelligible.
- Every screenshot, slide, or B-roll shot supports the current claim or viewer outcome.
- Dense proof visuals are held and highlighted long enough to be understood.
- Captions, callouts, and screen text have a clear hierarchy and do not collide.
- The intro confirms the promised outcome before diving into mechanism.
- Editorial graphics are readable, sourceable, and tied to the current claim.
- Maps, charts, document highlights, and screenshot focus sequences preserve context and do not mislead.
- Any Vox-inspired style avoids protected logos or unlicensed identity elements.
- Documentary typography has one clear primary source, claim, or label per graphic moment.
- Font choice supports tone without reducing source, map, chart, or caption readability.
- Switch-focus, parallax, and filmed-screen effects preserve one clear primary
  focus and readable evidence.
- Archival treatments are truthful for the source type and do not disguise
  generated or illustrative material as real evidence.
- Research overlay sequences reveal enough context to avoid cherry-picking.
