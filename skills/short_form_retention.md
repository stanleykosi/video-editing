# Short-Form Retention

## Purpose

Guide edits designed for TikTok, Reels, Shorts, and other fast-scroll feeds while
preventing retention tactics from damaging clarity, truth, or tone.

## When To Use This Skill

- Creating clips under 90 seconds.
- Turning long-form footage into high-retention shorts.
- Editing hooks, payoff density, pattern breaks, and rewatch potential.
- Choosing whether music, captions, motion, or eye-trace continuity can reduce drop-off.
- Front-loading polish into the first 30 seconds of a YouTube or short-form edit.
- Designing five-second claim hooks for feed-first shorts.
- Balancing high-energy hooks with readable rest beats.
- Building efficient Resolve short-form workflows that scale through presets.
- Building a Premiere short-form workflow from rough cut through captions, hook design, body visuals, post zooms, and music.
- Opening challenge or learning videos with a goal-gap loop.
- Reviewing AI-generated short-form clips, captions, and vertical layouts.
- Deciding when a long-form intro can delay the explicit promise without losing trust.
- Deciding whether a formal intro bumper belongs in a podcast, show, course, creator-brand, community, or repeatable social-series opener.
- Using music to raise perceived production value, tone, or retention without burying the premise.
- Planning premium After Effects Reels where each segment receives the lightest useful visual support.
- Checking viral-style audience fit, script strength, visual density, and publish threshold.
- Building slower human shorts where warmth, proof, and intentional imperfection carry retention.
- Designing short-form typography that improves the hook, proof, and CTA without slowing comprehension.
- Using simple CapCut keyframed zooms, overlays, and tracking to add movement to stale clips without losing clarity.
- Building high-energy CapCut/anime/action shorts where beat-matched speed, masks, transitions, SFX, and pre-rendering must support retention without hiding the action.
- Using graph-shaped CapCut zooms and motion blur as retention polish only when they preserve readable action and payoff.
- Using CapCut pro compositing effects as retention pattern breaks only when they
  clarify the point, preserve the hook, and remain readable on mobile.
- Using CapCut eye overlays and Pendulum movement as selected action accents
  rather than constant visual noise.

## Core Principles

- Open with a concrete tension, result, claim, question, visual surprise, or subject-first evidence.
- Remove context that can be inferred.
- Use pattern breaks only when attention is likely to dip.
- Keep the payoff close to the promise.
- Eye-trace continuity reduces viewer search time after cuts.
- Retention tactics must not make the meaning less truthful or less understandable.
- Confirm the promise early, but do not fully satisfy it before the story has begun.
- A visible near-miss or gap can create stronger retention than simply showing success.
- In long-form invitation intros, retention can come from specific questions, early loop closure, worldbuilding, and trust rather than immediate promise delivery.
- Music can improve retention when the cue, tone, and license are right, but it must not replace a clear reason to watch.
- The first 30 seconds deserve disproportionate care because more viewers see them.
- The first five seconds of a feed-first short should contain a claim, result, question, or proof that makes the viewer stop.
- Strong short-form hooks often come from desire, social proof, controversy, surprise, or disbelief, but the edit must actually support the claim.
- Trend research and niche-specific idea prompts can help source hooks; generic AI idea prompts usually create generic shorts.
- Title, thumbnail, intro, first line, and first proof beat should confirm the same promise.
- Formal intro bumpers are not default retention tools; use them only when brand identity, external proof, or outcome expectation creates trust faster than immediate content alone.
- Editing supplements the value; it should not hide a weak idea, slow cut, or unclear payoff.
- AI-generated clips must be checked for standalone context, start/end trims, captions, and layout.
- High-energy hooks can be followed by a brief readable rest beat so the explanation remains understandable.
- A simple postable short can be valid before advanced polish; pro quality comes from efficient repeatable style, not only effect count.
- Build the clean cut first; captions, hook design, body visuals, zoom polish, and music should sit on top of a readable timeline.
- Body visuals should scale with line importance: simple talking head, filler text card, B-roll text sequence, or full-screen animation.
- A high-quality Reel needs an editing signature: consistent fonts, colors, icon style, backgrounds, motion presets, and sound cues.
- Segment ideation should happen before animation so retention work follows the spoken concept instead of random effect availability.
- Different audiences want different editing density; do not apply one creator's viral grammar to a mismatched niche.
- The audience and idea gate come before retention polish: effects can package value, not create it from nothing.
- High standards do not mean zero pauses; the pause must earn its time.
- A minimally edited short can retain when the first seconds hook clearly and the body earns slower human attention.
- Imperfect human moments are retention assets only when they create connection and return cleanly to the story.
- Typography supports retention when one impact word reads instantly and the rest of the text stays secondary.
- Performance-led shorts should make the product, result, claim, or proof readable before typography style becomes noticeable.
- Keyframed motion can increase engagement in static clips only when it clarifies focus, supports a beat, or keeps the subject readable.
- High-energy effects retain only when the viewer can still understand the action, character, or payoff after speed, shake, glow, and SFX are added.
- Smooth graph zooms can raise perceived polish, but they do not fix an unclear hook, weak action beat, or unreadable crop.
- Pro effects retain attention when they create a real visual turn; they become
  clutter when the viewer cannot read the subject, caption, proof, or payoff.
- Tiny detail effects retain only when the viewer can see what changed without
  searching the frame.
- Movie recap shorts retain best as focused paragraph-sized units: one gripping
  narration beat, one verified source scene range, muted source audio, transformed
  fragments, and a short export rather than a full plot dump.

Applicability note: do not apply short-form speed habits blindly to long-form or sensitive documentary. Once a long-form viewer has committed, confident slower pacing can be the stronger retention tactic.
Contradiction note: the first 1-3 second promise rule remains preferred for feed-first shorts. For long-form documentary, a slower invitation can win when the opening image creates questions, the edit closes one loop early, and every delay builds world or proof.
Contradiction note: old-style logo bumpers should usually be skipped on ordinary mid-length YouTube videos. Prefer a formal intro only for podcasts, shows, courses, creator brands, communities, or repeatable social series where brand/proof/outcome packaging increases trust.

## Timing Rules

- The first 1-3 seconds should communicate why to keep watching in feed-first formats.
- The first five seconds should clarify the main claim, desire, proof, controversy, or question.
- Do not leave dead air in short-form unless it creates tension, humor, or clear emotional value.
- Pattern breaks should be timed to attention dips, not added on a fixed schedule.
- For long-form commitment, slow down after the viewer understands why the video matters.
- Goal-gap openings should show enough proof to earn trust while preserving the closure for later.
- In long-form invitation intros, close one concrete question loop before expanding the mystery.
- If the explicit promise is delayed, the viewer should still understand the tone, world, or conflict early.
- Music drops should land on a story, action, or emotional turn rather than becoming a random energy spike.
- Remove accidental dead air over 300 ms in shorts unless it creates tension, humor, or emotional value.
- Opening silence, awkward breath, or camera adjustment should be cut unless it sets the intended tone.
- AI short start and end points should be manually adjusted so the premise and payoff are complete.
- Do not spend all polish late in the edit while the opening remains slow or unclear.
- After a visually dense hook, include a clean rest beat before the next heavy visual stack if comprehension starts to suffer.
- For Resolve shorts, finish the clean vertical cut before adding Fusion effects.
- In Premiere short-form edits, remove silence and smooth rough dialogue cuts before generating captions or animating text.
- Designed hook text should finish establishing the claim or curiosity within the first five seconds.
- Filler cards, B-roll text, and full-screen animations should last only as long as the spoken concept remains useful.
- Do not begin AE animation until the short has a tight prepared cut and a segment-by-segment visual plan.
- Final captions, SFX, and music should be judged after the AE render is aligned because the visible motion changes readability and sync.
- In viral-style edits, trim dead flow but keep meaningful space when it improves story, joke timing, tension, or comprehension.
- For slower human shorts, the first 3-5 seconds still need motion, proof, face, or a clear reason to continue.
- Keep micro black/drop gaps under one second unless the pause is intentionally cinematic.
- Hook text hierarchy should be readable within the first five seconds, with the impact word visible quickly.
- Hold unscripted human interruptions only until the connection beat lands.
- For justified formal intros, cap social-series openings around 3-5 seconds and podcast/show openings around 10-15 seconds maximum.
- If a formal intro appears before the body, it should deliver brand, proof, and outcome before the viewer feels delayed.
- CapCut-style keyframed moves should settle or hold before the viewer needs to read the focus target.
- In high-energy action shorts, speed-match clips to visible beat markers before adding transition polish.
- Eye/power overlays should land on the transformation beat without delaying the payoff.
- Transition shake and distortion should settle before the next action or payoff needs to be read.
- Pendulum movement should not push a readable action payoff past the beat.
- Graph-shaped zooms should settle before the viewer needs to understand the subject, proof, or payoff.
- Motion blur should be added after the graph timing is locked and lowered if it smears the hook subject.
- Chapter cards and photo stacks should function as readable pattern breaks, not
  delays before the next useful line.
- Tracked stickers and green-screen VFX should clear once the emphasis beat lands.

## Motion Rules

- Motion should guide attention, clarify beats, or sell a transition.
- Avoid motion that makes captions unreadable or distracts from the promised payoff.
- In serious long-form, avoid fast effects that signal insecurity.
- Fast opening cuts need shared visual cues or a clear scale/overwhelm purpose; otherwise they read as noise.
- Use zooms, arrows, crops, or reframes when the important action is small or easy to miss.
- Keep one primary focal point; do not let captions, card text, overlays, and action all compete at once.
- Auto-reframed vertical layouts must keep active speakers, reactions, and proof visible.
- Use zooms to highlight important statements and tracking/reframing to follow subject movement.
- Leave enough body room in the crop for zoom-ins, zoom-outs, and subject tracking.
- Visualize abstract spoken concepts with text, icons, stock footage, GIFs, masks, or proof rather than more explanation.
- Put B-roll on key short-form lines and remove unwanted source audio.
- Use the lightest useful body visual: filler text for simple phrases, B-roll text for story/proof beats, and full-screen animation for high-value abstract concepts.
- Adjustment-layer zooms and blur are polish passes; add them only after the visual hierarchy and caption safety are already working.
- Choose each segment's support type deliberately: captions only, speaker plus text, B-roll/image/icon, full-screen animation, path/counter, or camera scene.
- Keep the visual style coherent across the Reel; random colors, icon styles, and backgrounds weaken retention by making the edit feel assembled from scraps.
- Script tags should determine where screen recordings, images, videos, text, overlays, and animations appear.
- A long simple dynamic zoom can be enough for an emotional section when the face, proof, or story stays compelling.
- Use center guides or eye-line continuity so early face cuts feel smooth rather than jumpy.
- Use typography contrast on the word that sells the claim, desire, disbelief, proof, or CTA; do not style every word equally.
- Premium intro motion should move through one focus at a time: brand or show identity, external proof or authority, then viewer outcome.
- Flicker, font swaps, logo moves, or 3D camera work belong only where they make the intro feel clearer and more premium without hiding the body promise.
- Keyframed zooms, overlays, and tilts should add movement to stale footage only when the first focus, proof, face, or action remains clear.
- Overlay and cutout animations should clear once their spoken concept is done so hook density does not bleed into the explanation.
- Dense AMV/action effects should be treated as hook or payoff emphasis, not a constant layer that prevents the viewer from reading the clip.
- Eye overlays and pendulum swings should be reserved for transformation, entrance, or transition accents that a cold viewer can understand.
- Heavy overlay pre-renders are useful only if they preserve timing, handles, caption safety, and editability in the main timeline.
- Extreme zoom stacks should be used for selected impact moments, not as a constant retention layer.
- Subject-sandwich and green-screen VFX effects should preserve the hook subject
  and not distract from the payoff.
- Photo stacks require planned negative space; if photos cover the face or proof,
  the retention tactic has failed.

## Sound Rules

- Music can increase retention when it fits tone and is licensed/platform-safe.
- SFX and music should not bury speech.
- Silence or ambience can be useful after commitment; do not fill every second out of fear.
- Use small pauses or music drops to make a near-miss register when the gap is real.
- Sound can make question loops feel intentional, but it must not hide the premise once VO returns.
- Audition tracks for retention-critical sections and choose the one that clarifies tone fastest.
- Duck music before key hook words, claims, or payoff lines.
- Music should match the voice, pace, and tone of the clip instead of trying to patch slow pacing.
- Vary repeated whooshes or hits in high-energy shorts.
- Repair quiet words before relying on captions to carry the hook.
- Use whooshes, hits, and reverse hits only where they match motion, text pops, anticipation, or reveal beats.
- Start dense whooshes low under dialogue and raise only if speech stays clear.
- Audition music under the finished short and choose by tone fit, speech clarity, and license/platform safety rather than trend status alone.
- Place whooshes, hits, typewriter cues, and reward sounds against the final visible motion so they support retention instead of drifting out of sync.
- Early music can help define vibe, but it must not hide weak voiceover pacing or licensing risk.
- Subtle foley can make human cutaways feel real, but it should stay below dialogue and match visible movement.
- Sound/cut contrast should build into a meaningful drop, reset, or calmer section.
- Intro SFX should be frame-locked to visible logo, text, flicker, underline, camera, cut, or transition events; remove decorative cues that do not sell a visible beat.
- Fast intro music can start near a 120 BPM-style pulse when it fits the brand, but proof quotes and body speech must stay intelligible.
- In short-form music beds, duck music before speech begins and restore only after the talking section clears.
- AMV/action SFX and voice lines should land on visible events and keep enough contrast that big moments still feel big.
- SFX cues should match the event type so power reveals, glass breaks, lightning entrances, and voice lines stay distinct.
- Music/SFX from app libraries or downloaded effects should be treated as
  temp-only until license/platform status is documented.

## Caption Rules

- Captions should be readable at phone size.
- Captions should not cover faces, products, maps, evidence, or action.
- Use captions to clarify, not to compensate for a weak hook.
- Do not caption away all curiosity in an invitation intro; clarify only what the viewer needs to stay oriented.
- Opening captions must be readable immediately at phone size.
- Captions should not cover title text, proof cards, faces, products, or the action being discussed.
- Remove filler-word caption emphasis when it distracts from the main point.
- One- or two-word subtitle chunks can fit high-energy social style when they remain readable.
- Reposition or simplify captions when visual text already carries the idea and another text layer would clutter the screen; suppress only with explicit user approval.
- One-word Premiere captions can fit high-energy Reels when the chunks are manually corrected and still readable.
- Convert captions to graphics only after timing, wording, placement, and collisions are fixed.
- Fade, reposition, or restyle captions over dense animation sections when the animation text already communicates the line; suppress only with explicit user approval.
- Constant text pops can become irritating; use emphasis text for important ideas, not every sentence.
- In warm human shorts, use restrained captions or soft fades when kinetic captions would fight the tone.
- Reposition or simplify captions when designed hook text, ad text, or proof labels already carry the same words; suppress only with explicit user approval.
- Break hook and caption text by phrase meaning so viewers do not have to decode random line wraps while scrolling.
- Reposition ordinary captions during designed intro text when brand, proof, or outcome words already carry the message; suppress only with explicit user approval.

## Color Rules

- Strong looks are fine when they support the format and subject.
- Avoid color choices that hide the hook subject, product, face, or proof.
- Brand colors should make the Reel feel intentional while keeping faces, captions, icons, and proof readable.
- Accent colors should mean priority, comparison, or brand; random color changes weaken short-form readability.
- Warm nostalgic looks can support hope or memory, but must preserve skin, proof, and caption contrast.

## Tool Implementation Notes

- For ffmpeg, render phone-scale previews and check the first 3 seconds, caption placement, and cut readability.
- For Remotion, model hook, setup, pattern breaks, and payoff as named sequences.
- Track open loops by ID so every opened goal has closure or reframing.
- For delayed long-form intros, track location, reality, motive, and conflict questions as loops, not only measurable goals.
- For CapCut/Premiere, compare fast and slower variants when the piece is long-form or sensitive.
- For music-led hooks, compare at least two track options at phone-speaker volume and check whether the premise remains clear.
- For AI clipping tools, store candidates with approved/rejected status, source start/end, layout, caption fixes, and context-risk notes.
- Render phone-size previews for the first 30 seconds and all exported shorts.
- For short-form claim planning, store claim type, first proof frame, payoff frame, and any trend or niche research note.
- For high-energy hooks, tag sections as hook, rest beat, accent, and payoff so density can be checked.
- For Resolve workflows, tag effects that should become Power Bin presets only after phone-size QC.
- For Premiere Reel workflows, tag phases as rough cut, caption style, caption animation, hook text design, body visual, adjustment zoom, and music audition.
- Store body visual choices with a reason: simple text, story B-roll, proof B-roll, full-screen object, or no visual support.
- For AE-heavy Reels, store per-segment fields for spoken concept, visual support type, assets needed, caption continuity policy, any user-approved suppression exception, SFX frame, and complexity level.
- Keep project/template assets organized so repeated output can preserve the same editing signature without rebuilding from scratch.
- For viral-style workflows, store target audience, idea gate status, script tags, asset needs, learned technique, and publish decision.
- For slow human shorts, store first-seconds hook type, human-interrupt purpose, micro-drop duration, foley cue frames, and warm-look notes.
- For typography-led shorts, store impactWords, fontFamilyRole, phraseLines, accentColor, safeRegion, and duplicateCaptionPolicy.
- For formal intros, store introAllowed, format, maxDuration, brandBeat, proofBeat, outcomeBeat, bodyStartFrame, and skipReason.
- For CapCut keyframed effects, store focusTarget, propertyKeyframes, holdFrames, captionSafeRegion, and cleanupFrame.

## Common Mistakes

- Using retention hacks that make serious material feel manipulative.
- Cutting too fast for viewers to scan the frame.
- Adding captions, effects, or music that bury the actual reason to watch.
- Treating connected-TV long-form viewers like fast-scroll viewers.
- Delivering the full promise too early and killing curiosity.
- Opening a loop that is never closed.
- Delaying the promise with atmosphere that does not build questions, proof, or trust.
- Creating vague mystery that the editor cannot name or close.
- Using music energy to hide a weak hook.
- Letting a recognizable track bury the actual reason to watch.
- Publishing AI-selected clips without watching them as standalone videos.
- Showing the full result before the viewer has a reason to keep watching.
- Starting with the mechanism or product before the viewer's desired outcome is clear.
- Stacking too many on-screen text layers in the hook.
- Using generic AI idea prompts instead of niche-specific research.
- Keeping hook-level visual density throughout the whole short.
- Making a big claim that the payoff does not support.
- Captioning every word when visual text already explains the idea.
- Treating a ten-hour flashy edit as automatically better than a clean repeatable preset workflow.
- Saving untested effects as reusable presets.
- Building hook graphics before the spoken cut is tight.
- Using full-screen animations on low-value lines that only needed a simple visual reset.
- Repeating the same adjustment zoom on every section until the edit feels automated.
- Opening After Effects before the spoken cut is tight or before each segment has a visual job.
- Mixing random asset styles, colors, and backgrounds until the Reel feels inconsistent.
- Leaving captions active over animation text that already carries the same words.
- Using a popular editing style that does not fit the target audience.
- Cutting every pause and removing the story's breathing room.
- Learning endlessly inside one video instead of publishing after core QC passes.
- Treating slower human pacing as a reason to ignore the first 3-5 seconds.
- Leaving a long black/drop gap that feels like an export mistake.
- Removing human texture that gave the short its connection value.
- Making hook typography attractive but too slow to understand during the first scroll beat.
- Highlighting filler words instead of the word that sells the claim or result.
- Adding a legacy logo bumper to every video and calling it retention.
- Building a premium-looking intro that never proves why the viewer should trust the creator or what they will gain.
- Adding zooms, stickers, tilts, or cutout motion to every stale section instead of fixing the edit's actual hook or pacing.
- Using intense shake, glow, and SFX to hide an action clip that was not cleanly timed to the beat.
- Keeping hook-level AMV density through the whole short until the payoff becomes hard to follow.
- Using graph zooms and motion blur to make a weak or unclear hook look busy.
- Repeating the same graph zoom stack on every clip until the edit feels templated.

## QC Checklist

- The opening clearly confirms the video promise.
- Captions are readable and do not cover key visuals.
- Music, motion, and styling support retention without burying the premise.
- The viewer can find the next key visual point immediately after smooth cuts.
- Artful openings are tested against clearer practical versions when broad reach matters.
- For long-form or sensitive subjects, fast cutting is justified by story rather than insecurity.
- The goal gap is real, visible, and later closed or intentionally reframed.
- For invitation intros, at least one early question loop is closed before the promise is delayed further.
- The delayed promise is tested against a clearer practical opening if the target audience is broad or feed-first.
- Music-led hooks preserve intelligible dialogue, readable captions, and clear premise.
- The strongest music cue lands on a retention-relevant visual or story beat.
- The first 30 seconds match the title and thumbnail promise.
- No short-form dead air longer than 300 ms remains unless it has a clear purpose.
- AI short candidates have complete premise, context, and payoff.
- Captions, title text, overlays, and proof visuals do not collide on mobile.
- The edit still has clear value when decorative effects are toggled off.
- The first five seconds contain a clear claim, result, proof, or question.
- Strong hook claims are supported by the later payoff.
- High-energy hooks have readable rest beats when the visual stack is dense.
- Statement zooms land on important lines rather than filler.
- Captions are removed or simplified where visual text already carries the idea.
- Simple vertical cut, captions, and B-roll are postable before Fusion polish is added.
- Reusable presets speed up output without making the short generic.
- Premiere one-word captions are corrected before graphic conversion and animation.
- Hook text has one clear keyword or phrase priority and does not duplicate captions.
- B-roll text sequences preserve the proof or action after dimming or desaturation.
- Adjustment-layer zooms affect only intended sections and keep captions, faces, UI, and proof safe.
- Each prepared segment has a visual support decision before animation starts.
- The editing signature is consistent across fonts, colors, icon style, backgrounds, motion, and SFX.
- Final SFX and captions are checked against the AE render, not only the rough Premiere timeline.
- Target audience and style lane are explicit before retention tactics are chosen.
- Pauses kept in the edit have a named purpose.
- Publish blockers are separated from optional improvements.
- Slow sections have a named connection, proof, emotion, or comprehension purpose.
